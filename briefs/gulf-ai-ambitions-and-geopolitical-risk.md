# Gulf AI Ambitions and Geopolitical Risk

### Hedging in the US-China Chip Race: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and the Regional Competition for AI Supremacy

**Muhammad Rafay Waqar** | Geopolitical Risk Assessment | September 2026
*Companion analysis to the [Gulf AI & Tech-Bloc Alignment Tracker](../README.md) — data and full sourcing in `data/curated/`.*

---

## Bottom Line Up Front

Gulf states are not choosing between Washington and Beijing on artificial intelligence — they are maximizing
ties with both simultaneously, and the United States is, for now, tolerating it. The UAE holds the most
favorable US chip-export status of any state in the region (BIS Country Group A:5, July 2026) while still
running Huawei radio equipment on live 5G networks. Saudi Arabia's chip access is real but capped and
bespoke, tied to a single sovereign vehicle (HUMAIN) rather than a country-wide rule. Smaller Gulf states
have no disclosed bilateral chip arrangement at all and are pursuing AI ambitions through lower-capital
channels — portfolio stakes, telecom partnerships, adoption frameworks — rather than compute buildout.
Pakistan and Turkey, outside the Gulf, show the deepest and most institutionally entrenched Chinese
technology integration in the comparison set, suggesting this hedging pattern is not Gulf-specific but a
general feature of how US security partners behave when a parallel China relationship is also available to
them. **The central risk for Western strategic interests is not that the Gulf will defect to China — it is
that Washington's bilateral, entity-specific approach to chip access is rewarding institutional capacity
(a single well-governed sovereign AI vehicle that can satisfy security and reporting conditions) as much as
strategic alignment, which is a narrower and more fragile basis for regional influence than it looks.**

## Key Judgments

1. **Gulf states, led by the UAE and Saudi Arabia, are pursuing simultaneous integration with both blocs
   rather than sequential alignment with one.** The clearest evidence is the UAE: the single most
   US-integrated state in this analysis by chip-export status, and simultaneously a state where e&
   deployed Huawei's MetaAAU radio hardware on a live 5G network in the same period. *(High confidence.)*

2. **Washington's post-May-2025 bilateral approach is tolerating continued Chinese commercial ties in
   exchange for entity-level compliance, not requiring a wholesale break with Chinese vendors.** The UAE's
   July 2026 Country Group A:5 upgrade came with a named, auditable list of approved end users (G42, Core42,
   eight US firms) rather than a blanket judgment on the UAE's overall posture toward China. This is a
   narrower ask than "pick a side," and it is one the UAE — and to a lesser extent Saudi Arabia — has been
   able to satisfy without changing its telecom vendor relationships. *(Moderate-high confidence.)*

3. **Saudi Arabia's chip access is real but structurally more fragile than the UAE's.** The Nov 2025
   authorization for HUMAIN (up to ~35,000 GB300-equivalent accelerators) is capped, entity-specific, and
   conditioned on security/reporting compliance — a bespoke arrangement, not a Country Group upgrade. Saudi
   Arabia did not receive the UAE's July 2026 treatment. This leaves Riyadh's compute ambitions more
   exposed to renegotiation risk than Abu Dhabi's if the bilateral relationship cools or a change in US
   administration priorities intervenes. *(Moderate confidence — this judgment could reverse quickly if
   Saudi Arabia receives its own Country Group upgrade, which is plausible given the pace of 2025-2026
   dealmaking.)*

4. **Smaller Gulf states are pursuing AI ambitions through a materially different, lower-capital-intensity
   strategy than the two regional leaders.** Qatar is building AI exposure through sovereign-fund portfolio
   stakes (Anthropic, Databricks, Cresta) rather than in-country compute; Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman show
   no disclosed compute or investment figures at the scale of Saudi Arabia/the UAE, and their AI governance
   apparatus (published strategies, but in most cases no dedicated regulator) lags accordingly. None has a
   disclosed bilateral BIS chip arrangement. *(Moderate confidence on the strategic characterization; low
   confidence on several individual country scores — see Limitations.)*

5. **Chinese technology integration is deepest not in the Gulf but in the two non-Gulf comparators.**
   Pakistan's Huawei relationship (entrenched since 1998, central to CPEC's digital backbone, formalized
   again via a 2025 PTA capacity-building MOU) and Turkey's multi-decade Turkcell-Huawei partnership
   (renewed at MWC 2025) are both deeper and more structurally embedded than any Gulf state's Huawei ties.
   Read alongside Turkey's 2020 CAATSA sanctioning over its S-400 purchase from Russia, this suggests
   hedging against exclusive US alignment is a broader pattern among Washington's security partners, not a
   Gulf-specific hedge against oil-transition anxiety. *(High confidence on the underlying facts; moderate
   confidence on the generalization, since Pakistan and Turkey's relationships with Washington differ from
   the Gulf states' in ways this analysis does not fully control for — see Alternative Analysis.)*

6. **The rescission of the Biden-era "AI Diffusion Rule" (13 May 2025) in favor of bilateral,
   government-to-government dealmaking has structurally advantaged states that can stand up a single,
   well-governed sovereign AI vehicle able to satisfy US security and reporting conditions.** HUMAIN and
   G42/MGX are the clearest examples: both are the direct counterparties named in their countries' access
   agreements. This rewards institutional capacity to build and govern such a vehicle at least as much as
   it rewards geopolitical alignment — a distinction with real implications for which states can plausibly
   follow the UAE/Saudi model and which cannot. *(Moderate confidence.)*

## Discussion

### The bilateral turn

The Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule (Jan 2025) would have imposed a worldwide license requirement
on advanced chip exports, sorting nearly every country into a small number of access tiers. Its rescission
four months later replaced a rules-based global framework with case-by-case, entity-specific dealmaking —
a shift that happened to land in the middle of the Gulf's most aggressive AI-investment year to date. The
UAE's and Saudi Arabia's 2025-2026 chip agreements are not incidental beneficiaries of this shift; they are
close to its paradigm case. Both countries had, by the time the rule was rescinded, already stood up (G42,
HUMAIN) or were standing up sovereign vehicles specifically built to be auditable, security-compliant
counterparties for exactly this kind of bilateral deal. Smaller Gulf states without an equivalent vehicle
have, correspondingly, gotten nothing — not because Washington has judged them less aligned, but because
there is no entity on their side positioned to receive a comparable authorization.

### Two-track hedging as the empirical pattern, not a hypothesis

It is tempting to read continued Huawei ties in the UAE and Saudi Arabia as legacy relationships in the
process of being wound down. The data available for this analysis does not support that reading yet: the
e&-Huawei MetaAAU deployment and the Ooredoo Group's Huawei 5G-A partnerships across Kuwait and Oman are
2024-2025 agreements, not expiring 2010s contracts. States with the most to lose from a US chip
relationship breaking down are, at present, not visibly reducing their Chinese vendor exposure. Whether that
changes is one of the more important open questions in this analysis (see Outlook).

### A governance divergence that tracks capacity, not alignment

The UAE (Minister of State for AI since 2017, a 2025 Regulatory Intelligence Office) and Saudi Arabia (SDAIA,
established 2019, a binding AI Adoption Framework since 2024) have built the region's only mature AI
governance apparatus. Every other GCC state has published a national AI strategy — a low bar — but only
Bahrain shows movement toward binding AI-specific regulation, and Oman's policy was still reported as
"finalizing" as of this analysis. This divergence in institutional maturity plausibly explains the chip-access
gap as well as any explicitly strategic factor: Washington has bilateral partners to negotiate with in Abu
Dhabi and Riyadh in a way it currently does not in Doha, Manama, Kuwait City, or Muscat.

## Alternative Analysis

Three competing explanations for the observed pattern were considered. This analysis favors Hypothesis A,
with B as a live possibility worth tracking rather than ruled out.

- **Hypothesis A — Deliberate hedging.** Gulf states are consciously diversifying technology-bloc exposure
  to protect against both US supply disruption and the risk of being abandoned if Washington's priorities
  shift, mirroring the same logic that has driven their reserve-currency and defense-partner
  diversification for decades. *Best supported by the evidence*: the pattern holds across multiple
  independent domains (chip access, telecom vendors, sovereign investment) and multiple countries with
  different individual relationships to Washington, which is harder to explain as coincidence than as
  strategy.

- **Hypothesis B — Transitional sequencing, not hedging.** Current Chinese vendor relationships are legacy
  commercial arrangements that will be wound down once secure, sufficient US chip supply is fully in place;
  what looks like hedging is really just the lag between signing new US deals and exiting old Chinese ones.
  *Cannot be ruled out*: the UAE's and Saudi Arabia's US chip relationships are both less than a year old
  at time of writing, which is not enough time to judge whether Huawei exposure is declining in response.
  This is the single most important thing the next 12-18 months of data will resolve — see Outlook.

- **Hypothesis C — Uncoordinated commercial opportunism.** Sovereign funds and state telecom operators are
  making deal-by-deal decisions optimizing for cost and capability, with no coordinated bloc strategy behind
  them; "hedging" is an analyst's narrative imposed after the fact on what is really just a series of
  independent commercial choices. *Least supported*: sovereign AI vehicles in both Saudi Arabia and the UAE
  report through structures with direct state ownership and, in the UAE's case, a cabinet-level AI
  authority — a degree of centralized coordination that argues against pure opportunism, even if it doesn't
  fully rule it out at the telecom-vendor level.

## Implications for Western Strategic Interests

- **Chip diversion risk is a Gulf-specific concern precisely because Gulf states are the most successful
  bilateral partners.** The Chip Security Act's location-verification mandate (introduced May 2025, moving
  through Congress as of this writing) exists because of documented smuggling cases elsewhere (over $1
  billion in controlled Nvidia chips diverted to China in a three-month period cited in congressional
  testimony); a large, concentrated, capped allocation like HUMAIN's is exactly the kind of arrangement
  that mandate is designed to audit. Enforcement outcomes here will be a live test of whether the bilateral
  model can be trusted at scale.
- **US leverage over the Gulf is currently narrower than "chip access" suggests.** Washington's ask has been
  entity-level compliance, not bloc alignment — which means the leverage US chip access buys is leverage
  over specific vehicles (HUMAIN, G42) and their compliance behavior, not leverage over Gulf states'
  broader foreign-policy or technology-vendor choices. Treating chip diplomacy as a proxy for broader
  alignment risks overestimating how much influence it actually secures.
- **Interdependence now runs in both directions.** Microsoft's $15.2bn UAE commitment and OpenAI's
  Stargate UAE partnership mean US firms now have capital and infrastructure exposure in the Gulf, not
  just the other way around — a dynamic that gives Gulf partners some leverage of their own in future
  negotiations, and one this analysis did not have space to model in the underlying tracker's scoring.
- **Regional stability implications are more about diversification than about bloc politics.** Non-oil
  diversification and AI-linked capital inflows plausibly reduce medium-term rentier-state fragility risk
  in Saudi Arabia and the UAE (the MENASA Risk Monitor's broader sovereign-risk framework is the more
  appropriate tool for tracking that channel specifically); this brief's scope is the technology-bloc
  question, not a full stability assessment.

## Outlook: Indicators to Watch

- **Does Saudi Arabia receive a Country-Group-style upgrade analogous to the UAE's July 2026 move?** This
  would be the strongest signal that the bilateral model is scaling beyond a single favored partner.
- **Does Huawei's footprint in UAE or Saudi telecom infrastructure measurably decline following their
  respective chip-access gains?** This is the most direct test between Hypothesis A (hedging persists) and
  Hypothesis B (transitional sequencing) above.
- **Chip Security Act enforcement outcomes**, once the location-verification mandate is implemented,
  specifically involving Gulf-allocated chips.
- **Whether Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, or Oman secure their own bilateral BIS arrangement** — the clearest
  sign that the "sovereign vehicle capacity" explanation (Key Judgment 6) generalizes beyond the two current
  leaders.
- **China's rare-earth and critical-mineral export controls (tightened further in early 2026)** and whether
  they extend to components used in Gulf-bound AI infrastructure, which would raise the cost of the
  "maximalist hedging" strategy directly.

## Methodology and Limitations

This assessment draws on the country-level dataset built for the companion
[Gulf AI & Tech-Bloc Alignment Tracker](../README.md) (`data/curated/*.csv`), covering Saudi Arabia, UAE,
Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Pakistan, and Turkey. Every factual claim above traces to a cited,
dated source in that dataset; confidence levels in the Key Judgments reflect both the strength of that
sourcing and the size of the inferential step from evidence to judgment, following standard analytic
tradecraft practice (a claim can be well-sourced and still carry only moderate confidence, if the judgment
built on it requires assuming more than the evidence directly shows).

**This analysis inherits the tracker's data-thinness limitations directly.** Several inputs behind the
smaller Gulf states' characterization in Key Judgment 4 are marked `Low` confidence in the underlying
dataset — no BIS rule or entity authorization was found for Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, or Oman in this research
pass, and their export-control scores rest on analyst judgment against a documented rubric rather than a
real designation. Turkey's governance-maturity assessment likewise rests on general awareness of its
TÜBİTAK-led AI strategy rather than a freshly verified 2025-2026 source. None of the Key Judgments above
depend solely on a `Low`-confidence input, but a reader relying on this brief for a specific claim about
Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, or Turkey's governance apparatus should check the relevant row in
`data/curated/` first.

This is a research/portfolio product, not a commissioned or institutional assessment, and should be read
accordingly.
