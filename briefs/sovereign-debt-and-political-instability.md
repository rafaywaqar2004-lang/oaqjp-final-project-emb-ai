# Sovereign Debt and Political Instability

### Feedback Loops in South Asian Economies: Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh's Divergent Paths Through Fiscal Crisis and Regime Change

**Muhammad Rafay Waqar** | Geopolitical Risk Assessment | September 2026

drawn from that tool's `scored_data.csv` and `driver_data.csv`; qualitative sourcing from its curated

## Bottom Line Up Front

South Asia's three most fiscally stressed economies -- Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh -- each confirm
that sovereign debt distress and political instability reinforce each other, but they do not confirm a
single causal arrow, and treating "feedback loop" as a one-way, fiscal-crisis-causes-political-collapse
story would misread two of the three cases. Sri Lanka is the clean, textbook version: debt default in April
2022 produced shortages that produced mass protest that produced a government's collapse within months.
Pakistan is the opposite pattern in duration if not in kind: chronic fiscal fragility (70.1% debt-to-GDP,
a 50.7/100 risk score, worsening year-over-year) has never tipped into full default, sustained instead by
serial IMF programs and by China, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE repeatedly rolling over bilateral debt -- while
the political response to instability has been constitutional centralization around the military rather
than collapse. Bangladesh inverts the sequence entirely: an authoritarian government fell in August 2024
over a policy grievance and a violent crackdown, with a corruption-driven reserve drain as background
condition rather than proximate trigger; a full-blown fiscal crisis did not need to occur first. **The
region's real lesson for anyone using a governance index to anticipate instability is that Worldwide
Governance Indicators-style scores are a weaker predictor of near-term crisis than debt trajectory,
reserve buffers, and the reliability of external creditor relationships combined -- and that the same
underlying stress can resolve as default, as managed chronic fragility, or as abrupt political rupture,
depending on factors a purely economic model won't capture.**

## Key Judgments

1. **Sri Lanka's 2022 sovereign default is the clearest South Asian case of a direct, compressed
   fiscal-to-political feedback loop.** Debt distress produced fuel, gas, medicine, and food shortages;
   the resulting Aragalaya protest movement forced Prime Minister Mahinda Rajapaksa's resignation within
   weeks of the country's first-ever sovereign default in April 2022. The sequence from default to regime
   change took months, not years. *(High confidence.)*

2. **Pakistan shows a structurally different pattern: chronic fragility managed indefinitely rather than
   resolved.** Its debt-to-GDP (70.1%, 2026) and risk score (50.7, worsening 1.3 points year-over-year) are
   both worse than Sri Lanka's current post-restructuring readings, yet Pakistan has never defaulted --
   sustained by a 2013 EFF, a 2019 EFF, a 2023 Stand-By Arrangement, and a current Extended Fund Facility,
   alongside China holding roughly $29bn in CPEC-linked bilateral debt with repeated rollovers (a $2bn
   rollover in March 2025, a further ~$3.4bn in June 2025) and Saudi/UAE central-bank deposit rollovers
   bridging financing gaps between IMF disbursements. The political response to repeated instability has
   trended toward centralization rather than collapse: the 26th Amendment (Oct 2024) curbed judicial
   independence, and the 27th Amendment (Nov 2025) created a life-tenured Chief of Defence Forces role
   with president-like legal immunity. This is a "manage, don't resolve" equilibrium, not a stable one.
   *(Moderate-high confidence.)*

3. **Bangladesh's August 2024 collapse of the Hasina government inverts the usual sequencing.** Political
   overreach (a violent crackdown on student protests over public-sector hiring quotas) and a
   corruption-driven reserve drain (foreign reserves fell from roughly $48bn in Aug 2021 to about $22bn by
   mid-2024, compounded by a reported $3.1bn loan-default scandal) preceded rather than followed acute
   political rupture -- a full fiscal crisis was not a precondition for the government's fall. Two
   consecutive sovereign downgrades (Moody's, 2023 and Nov 2024) tracked the political deterioration more
   than they preceded it. *(Moderate confidence.)*

4. **Political-stability scores alone are a weaker predictor of near-term crisis outcome than debt
   trajectory and reserve buffers combined.** Pakistan's WGI political-stability reading (-2.06) is far
   worse than Sri Lanka's current, post-crisis figure (-0.35) or Bangladesh's (-1.46) -- yet Pakistan is the
   one of the three that has not defaulted. Reserve cover and the reliability of external rollover partners
   explain more of the divergence in outcome than the governance indicator does on its own. *(Moderate
   confidence -- three cases is not a statistically robust sample; this is a pattern worth testing against
   more of the MENASA Risk Monitor's other 31 countries, not a proven relationship.)*

5. **Low debt-to-GDP is not a safety signal in this region -- it can indicate exclusion from capital
   markets rather than fiscal health.** Afghanistan's 8% debt-to-GDP is the lowest of the eight South Asian
   economies in the MENASA Risk Monitor's coverage, yet it carries the worst composite risk score in the
   set (58.1) -- a reflection of the Taliban government's near-total exclusion from international lending
   rather than underlying strength. A reader using debt-to-GDP as a standalone risk screen would rank
   Afghanistan safest of the eight; it is the riskiest. *(High confidence on the fact; moderate confidence
   on how far the general point extends beyond this one clear case.)*

6. **External creditor relationships function as a de facto stabilizer that domestic-governance indicators
   don't capture.** China's willingness to roll over Pakistan's bilateral debt, and China, Japan, and
   India's coordinated roles in Sri Lanka's post-default Official Creditor Committee restructuring, have
   mattered to near-term stability at least as much as either country's domestic governance quality. This
   is a channel a governance-indicator-only risk model would miss entirely. *(Moderate confidence.)*

## Discussion

### Three variants of the same underlying stress

All three countries share the same underlying vulnerability -- twin fiscal and external-balance pressure,
compounded by governance weakness -- but the variable that seems to determine *how* that stress resolves is
not captured by the fiscal or governance data alone. Sri Lanka had no external patron willing or able to
backstop it indefinitely and let reserves run out entirely before restructuring; Pakistan has three (China,
Saudi Arabia, the UAE) willing to keep rolling over debt rather than forcing a reckoning; Bangladesh's
political system broke under an unrelated grievance before its fiscal deterioration reached Sri Lanka-level
severity. The same stress, three different release valves.

### The IMF program as stabilizer, not solution

Every country examined here is either in or has recently exited an IMF program: Pakistan's current EFF
(preceded by the 2023 SBA and two prior EFFs), Sri Lanka's 48-month EFF approved March 2023 (its 17th IMF
program), and Bangladesh's $4.7bn program approved January 2023 (the first Asian country to access the
Resilience and Sustainability Facility). None of these programs resolved the underlying structural
weaknesses their countries face -- Pakistan's history of serial programs is the clearest evidence that an
IMF arrangement buys time and averts an acute crisis without addressing what keeps producing the next one.
Markets appear to price this distinction: all three major rating agencies upgraded Pakistan's sovereign
rating through 2025 on the back of IMF-anchored fiscal consolidation, even as the same period saw
significant democratic backsliding (the 26th and 27th Amendments) -- a real tension for any Western
policymaker treating "IMF program on track" as a proxy for "improving governance." The two are not the same
thing, and Pakistan is the clearest case in this set of them diverging.

### Governance data lags the events it's meant to anticipate

The WGI-based political-stability indicators used in the MENASA Risk Monitor's own composite score are
annual and backward-looking -- exactly the limitation that tool's own README flags. Bangladesh's
August 2024 collapse is a useful stress test of that limitation: the WGI data available at the time would
have captured Bangladesh as an increasingly centralized, but not acutely unstable, one-party state. The
speed and scale of the collapse once triggered was not something a governance-indicator trend alone would
have flagged as imminent.

## Alternative Analysis

Three framings for how fiscal crisis and political instability relate were tested against the three case
studies. No single framing fits all three -- which is itself the finding, not a failure to find one.

- **Framing A -- Fiscal crisis as primary driver, political instability as consequence.** The classic
  IMF-austerity-riot model: debt distress and its remedies (subsidy cuts, currency devaluation, tax
  increases) generate the material hardship that produces unrest and, eventually, regime change.
  *Best fits Sri Lanka*: the causal chain from default to Aragalaya to Rajapaksa's resignation is direct
  and well-documented, with little ambiguity about sequence.

- **Framing B -- Political instability/governance failure as primary driver, fiscal crisis as consequence
  or accelerant.** Authoritarian overreach, corruption, and repression are the root cause; economic
  deterioration compounds the resulting instability without being its origin. *Best fits Bangladesh*: the
  proximate trigger (a hiring-quota policy grievance and a violent crackdown) had no direct fiscal content,
  even though reserve depletion and a loan-default scandal had already begun eroding the government's
  economic credibility beforehand.

- **Framing C -- Mutually reinforcing with no clean causal priority; a chronic rather than acute
  dynamic.** Fiscal fragility and governance weakness compound each other repeatedly over years without a
  single trigger event resolving (or ending) the relationship. *Best fits Pakistan*: fourteen years of
  historical-context entries in the underlying dataset show repeated crisis-and-management cycles (2013,
  2019, 2022, 2023) with no single point where one factor clearly caused the other -- and no resolution,
  just continuation.

## Implications for Regional Stability and Western Strategic Interests

- **IMF conditionality's effectiveness depends on a government's political capacity to sustain it, which
  the Fund's own frameworks don't score.** Pakistan's rating upgrades mid-program alongside its
  democratic backsliding is the clearest evidence that "on-track with the IMF" and "improving governance"
  are separable outcomes -- a distinction worth holding onto for any Western policy that treats IMF
  program compliance as a broader stability signal.
- **China's creditor posture differs meaningfully between the two cases where it matters most.** In
  Pakistan, China acts as a rollover lender of last resort, repeatedly extending rather than restructuring
  bilateral debt. In Sri Lanka, China participated in a formal, coordinated restructuring alongside Japan
  and India via the Official Creditor Committee. The same creditor, two different postures -- worth
  tracking as a leading indicator of how Beijing might treat future distressed borrowers elsewhere in the
  region.
- **Bangladesh's export concentration gives trade policy outsized leverage over its post-crisis
  trajectory.** Ready-made garments are roughly 80% of Bangladesh's exports; the negotiated reduction of a
  threatened 35% US tariff to 19-20% (signed Feb 2026) is a live example of how a single Western trade
  decision can matter more to a post-instability economy's recovery than most domestic policy choices
  available to its interim government.
- **A purely quantitative early-warning model would have missed Bangladesh and been late on Pakistan.**
  This has a direct methodological implication for the MENASA Risk Monitor itself: the backward-looking,
  annual WGI-based composite score is a reasonable screening tool but not a leading indicator for the kind
  of abrupt political rupture Bangladesh experienced -- the tool's own Live Conflicts tab (qualitative,
  more frequently updated) is the more appropriate complement for that purpose, not the composite score
  alone.

## Outlook: Indicators to Watch

- **Bangladesh's February 12, 2026 parliamentary elections and constitutional referendum** -- the first
  national vote since Hasina's ouster, and the clearest near-term test of whether the interim government's
  fiscal-discipline pivot (a nominally shrinking national budget, IMF-aligned restraint) survives a return
  to elected government.
- **Whether Pakistan's IMF-anchored rating upgrades continue** even as the 27th Amendment's centralization
  of power around the Chief of Defence Forces role is tested by the next political flashpoint.
- **Sri Lanka's continuing IMF review cycle** (a combined fifth/sixth review reached staff-level agreement
  in April 2026) as the test case for whether a completed restructuring actually produces durable fiscal
  stability rather than a pause before the next distress cycle.
- **Any further China, Saudi, or UAE rollover requirement for Pakistan** before its current EFF concludes
  -- a recurrence would reinforce Key Judgment 2's "manage, don't resolve" characterization; a genuine
  reduction in rollover dependency would weaken it.
- **Afghanistan's continued exclusion from international capital markets** and whether any partial
  reintegration occurs -- the clearest test of whether Key Judgment 5's "low debt as market exclusion, not
  strength" reading continues to hold.

## Methodology and Limitations

This assessment's quantitative layer (risk scores, debt-to-GDP, political-stability and governance
indicators, reserves) is drawn directly from the MENASA Risk Monitor's `scored_data.csv`, current as of
that tool's most recent scheduled refresh. Its qualitative layer (the IMF-program histories, the Aragalaya
and Bangladesh-uprising narratives, the creditor-relationship summaries) is drawn from that tool's curated
`context_data.py`, itself sourced from IMF press releases, Reuters/Bloomberg/AP wire reporting, and rating
agency actions, fact-checked as of August 2026 per that tool's own methodology note.

**This brief inherits the Risk Monitor's own stated limitations.** Its composite score is annual and
backward-looking by design (see Implications, above, for where that specifically mattered in Bangladesh's
case); debt-to-GDP coverage across its full 34-country set is sparse and the years of the underlying figure
vary by country (Sri Lanka's cited debt-to-GDP dates to 2015, Nepal's to 2021, Bhutan's to 2020 -- readers
should treat cross-country debt comparisons in this brief as directional, not perfectly time-matched). The
Risk Monitor's own README is explicit that its weights are "a transparent, reasonable starting point -- not
a backtested or econometrically validated model," and that caveat applies with equal force to any
conclusion drawn from its data here.

**This brief examines three of the region's eight South Asian economies in depth** (Pakistan, Sri Lanka,
Bangladesh) with four more used only for comparative data points (India, Nepal, Bhutan, Maldives,
Afghanistan) -- not because the latter group is less analytically interesting, but because the underlying
dataset's qualitative depth is concentrated on the three countries that have experienced the most acute
fiscal-political stress in the period covered. A fuller version of this brief would extend the same
case-study depth to Nepal's frequent government turnover and the Maldives' India-China geopolitical debt
tension, both flagged here only as directions for a future pass.
