# Sovereign Debt and Political Instability

### Three Case Studies in Fiscal-Political Feedback Loops: Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh

**Muhammad Rafay Waqar** | Geopolitical Risk Assessment | September 2026
*Companion analysis to the [MENASA Risk Monitor](https://menasa-risk-monitor.onrender.com) -- quantitative
data drawn from that tool's `scored_data.csv` and `driver_data.csv`; qualitative sourcing from its curated
`context_data.py` historical-context, credit-rating, and current-government records.*

---

## Bottom Line Up Front

South Asia's three most fiscally stressed economies each confirm that sovereign debt distress and
political instability reinforce each other -- but reading the three cases in sequence, rather than as a
single blended finding, shows they do not confirm a single causal arrow. **Sri Lanka** is the clean,
textbook version: default in April 2022 produced shortages, mass protest, and two presidential
resignations within four months. **Pakistan** is chronic rather than acute: fourteen years of recurring
IMF programs and near-crises without ever tipping into full default, now compounded by two new 2025
military-adjacent conflicts (with India, and separately with the Afghan Taliban) layered on top of its
usual fiscal fragility, and a political response that has trended toward formal military constitutional
primacy rather than collapse. **Bangladesh** inverts the sequence outright: a government fell in August
2024 over a policy grievance and a violent crackdown, with fiscal deterioration as background condition
rather than proximate trigger -- and the political system has kept reorganizing since, with a new president
elected by Parliament as recently as August 2026. **The throughline across all three is that none of them
has actually resolved anything -- Sri Lanka's restructuring, Pakistan's rating upgrades, and Bangladesh's
interim-government fiscal discipline are each better described as the current phase of an still-unsettled
story than as an ending.**

## Case I: Pakistan -- Chronic Fragility, Managed Indefinitely

**Current standing:** Risk score 50.7/100 (Moderate Risk, worsening 1.3 points year-over-year);
debt-to-GDP 70.1%; sovereign ratings B- (S&P) / B3 (Moody's, upgraded from Caa1 in August 2026) / CCC-
(Fitch) -- still solidly speculative-grade despite the recent upgrades.

**The pattern, in sequence:** Pakistan's post-2013 history is a near-unbroken cycle of IMF engagement.
A $6.64bn Extended Fund Facility (September 2013) is, by the IMF's own retrospective assessment, the only
one of Pakistan's programs to have run its full course successfully. A second EFF (July 2019, ~$6bn) was
repeatedly stalled by missed fiscal and energy-pricing targets. Both political and physical shocks followed
in 2022: Imran Khan's April removal by no-confidence vote triggered a political crisis just as catastrophic
monsoon floods (June-October 2022) submerged roughly a third of the country, causing an estimated $30bn in
damages against reconstruction needs exceeding $16bn, and shaving about 2.2 percentage points off FY22
GDP. Reserves fell to roughly $9bn (under two months of import cover) and the rupee was devalued by about
28%. A $3bn Stand-By Arrangement (July 2023) arrived after months of brinkmanship, followed by a fourth
program -- a $7bn, 37-month EFF (September 2024) that for the first time formalized provincial fiscal
targets alongside federal ones.

**The political response has been centralization, not collapse.** The May 2023 riots following Imran
Khan's arrest saw PTI supporters attack over 40 military installations. The February 2024 general election
produced a hung parliament amid rigging allegations. Parliament then passed two consecutive constitutional
amendments consolidating power away from elected/judicial checks: the 26th Amendment (October 2024)
stripped the Supreme Court of suo motu powers and gave a parliamentary committee effective control over
Chief Justice selection; the 27th Amendment (November 2025) created a life-tenured Chief of Defence Forces
role -- held concurrently by Army Chief Asim Munir, elevated to Field Marshal -- with command over the
nuclear Strategic Plans Division and president-like immunity, removable only by a two-thirds majority
versus the simple majority needed to remove an elected Prime Minister. This is, in a literal constitutional
sense, a formal entrenchment of military primacy over elected government.

**2025 added a geopolitical dimension largely absent from the 2013-2024 story.** In May 2025, India's
Operation Sindoor missile strikes (following the Pahalgam terror attack) triggered four days of
cross-border exchanges; Pakistan's KSE-100 index fell roughly 13% cumulatively and the closed Attari-Wagah
trade corridor cost an estimated $132-168m. Separately, a new border war with the Afghan Taliban government
erupted from October 2025; a Qatar-mediated ceasefire collapsed within a month, Pakistan's exports to
Afghanistan fell roughly 56% in early 2026, and Pakistan is estimated to have lost nearly $850m from border
closures through May 2026. Neither conflict is captured by the IMF-program cycle that has otherwise defined
Pakistan's sovereign-risk story -- both are new, non-fiscal shock vectors layered on top of the chronic
fragility.

**Case-specific judgments:**
- *(Moderate-high confidence)* Pakistan's rating upgrades through 2025-2026 reflect real IMF-anchored
  fiscal consolidation, but the country remains firmly speculative-grade and structurally dependent on
  continued rollovers from China, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE -- stabilization, not resolution.
- *(High confidence)* The 26th and 27th Amendments constitute a genuine, formal shift in where sovereign
  power sits, with direct relevance to future policy predictability: a government whose economic
  decision-making authority is checked by an unaccountable military office is a different counterparty for
  creditors and the IMF than a fully civilian one, regardless of what the fiscal indicators say in isolation.
- *(Moderate confidence)* The 2025 India and Afghanistan conflicts demonstrate that Pakistan's risk profile
  now has two live geopolitical shock vectors beyond its traditional fiscal-crisis axis -- worth tracking
  as independent risk channels, not folded into the same story as the IMF-program cycle.

## Case II: Sri Lanka -- The Clean Default-to-Collapse Sequence

**Current standing:** Risk score 38.8/100 (Moderate Risk, essentially flat year-over-year at +0.3);
debt-to-GDP 73.5% (2015 reference year -- the most recent World Bank figure available); ratings CCC+
(S&P) / Caa1 (Moody's) / CCC+ (Fitch, upgraded from Restricted Default in December 2024 after bond
restructuring).

**The pattern, in sequence:** Two pre-crisis episodes set up the eventual collapse. The 99-year Hambantota
port lease to China Merchants Port Holdings (finalized December 2017, $1.12bn) became the emblematic
"debt-trap diplomacy" case study in Western commentary, even though the lease proceeds bolstered reserves
rather than being tied to a loan default. The Easter Sunday bombings (April 2019) killed over 200 people
and devastated tourism-dependent GDP, with monthly arrivals collapsing from 166,975 in March to 37,800 in
May. Gotabaya Rajapaksa won the November 2019 presidential election on a security-and-reform platform;
his government's April 2021 sudden nationwide ban on chemical fertilizers and pesticides -- an attempted
full organic-farming transition -- combined with a 2019 tax-cut package to accelerate the fiscal
deterioration that followed.

**The collapse itself was fast and total.** The Aragalaya ("the struggle") protest movement erupted in
March 2022 over acute fuel, gas, medicine, and food shortages. Sri Lanka suspended external debt payments
in April 2022 -- its first-ever sovereign default. Prime Minister Mahinda Rajapaksa resigned in May;
President Gotabaya Rajapaksa fled the country by military jet on July 13 and resigned from exile in
Singapore the next day, the first Sri Lankan president to leave office mid-term. Parliament elected Ranil
Wickremesinghe president a week later (July 20) -- an unprecedented but constitutionally clean transfer of
power that avoided full institutional breakdown and produced the technocratic government that subsequently
negotiated the recovery.

**Recovery has been genuine, not cosmetic.** The IMF approved a 48-month, ~$3bn EFF in March 2023 (Sri
Lanka's 17th IMF program). External debt restructuring proceeded in stages through 2023-2024: a domestic
debt operation, then Agreements in Principle with China EXIM Bank and an Official Creditor Committee
co-chaired by India, Japan, and France. By end-2024, real GDP had recovered about 40% of the output lost
during the 2018-2023 crisis, with growth averaging 4.3% since the Q3 2023 recovery began. Fitch's upgrade
out of Restricted Default (December 2024) followed the completed bond restructuring. Politically, the
September-November 2024 elections were historic: Anura Kumara Dissanayake of the Marxist-rooted National
People's Power alliance won the presidency, and NPP's snap parliamentary win (159 of 225 seats) gave it an
unprecedented two-thirds supermajority -- a complete break from Rajapaksa-Wickremesinghe-era politics.

**Case-specific judgments:**
- *(High confidence)* The full sequence from default to dual presidential exits took roughly four months
  (April-July 2022) -- the fastest and most complete fiscal-to-political transmission of the three cases,
  with almost no ambiguity about causal direction.
- *(High confidence)* Sri Lanka's recovery trajectory is real and measurable (GDP recovery, rating
  upgrades, a completed restructuring), which distinguishes it from Pakistan's "managed but unresolved"
  pattern -- though at CCC+/Caa1 it remains deep in distressed territory, not a clean bill of health.
- *(Moderate confidence)* The NPP's two-thirds supermajority is a genuinely unusual political outcome for
  Sri Lanka and represents a real repudiation of the political establishment that presided over the
  default -- worth watching as a test case for whether a mandate this large translates into durable
  reform rather than the concentration-of-power pattern seen in Pakistan.

## Case III: Bangladesh -- Political Rupture Preceding Fiscal Crisis

**Current standing:** Risk score 49.4/100 (Moderate Risk, improving 2.3 points year-over-year);
debt-to-GDP 41.8% -- the lowest of the three cases by a wide margin; ratings B+ (S&P, negative outlook,
July 2026) / B2 (Moody's, negative outlook) / B+ (Fitch, downgraded from BB-, negative outlook). As of
August 2026, Bangladesh has a new head of state: Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir was elected the 23rd
President by Parliament, succeeding Mohammed Shahabuddin, who resigned mid-2026 citing health concerns --
the political reorganization triggered in August 2024 is still actively unfolding two years later.

**The pattern, in sequence -- and it runs in reverse of Sri Lanka's.** Reserve deterioration began well
before the political break: gross foreign exchange reserves fell from a peak of roughly $48bn (August
2021) to about $33bn by end-2022, driven by an import surge, falling remittances, and export weakness,
compounded by a reported $3.1bn loan-default scandal. Fitch and Moody's both downgraded Bangladesh in May
2023 as reserves continued falling (to $21.8bn by mid-2024, roughly 3.3 months of import cover). The IMF
approved a $4.7bn, 42-month program in January 2023 -- Bangladesh was the first Asian country to access
the Fund's new Resilience and Sustainability Facility. None of this, on its own, was acute enough to
topple a government that had held power for fifteen years.

**The actual trigger was a policy dispute with no direct fiscal content.** Student-led protests against a
reinstated civil-service hiring quota escalated dramatically after July 15, 2024, when ruling-party-
affiliated student wing members attacked peaceful demonstrators at Dhaka University; violence spread
nationwide, killing over 200 people within ten days (75 in a single day, July 19), prompting a nationwide
internet blackout and an army/paramilitary shoot-on-sight curfew that also halted ready-made-garment (RMG)
export operations mid-IMF-program. Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina was ousted on August 5, 2024, ending 15
years of Awami League rule; Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus was sworn in three days later to lead an interim
government.

**The interim period has been defined by deliberate fiscal restraint layered onto ongoing political
reorganization.** December 2024 saw the interim government simultaneously seek additional IMF funding
while holding the fiscal deficit to 3.6% of GDP, a pivot toward Fund-aligned discipline that set up the
historic mid-2025 national budget -- the first nominal budget contraction in Bangladesh's history. RMG
exports nonetheless grew (to $39.35bn in FY2024-25, +8.84% y/y, still roughly 80% of total exports), giving
Washington real leverage: a threatened 35% US tariff was negotiated down to 19-20% in a February 2026 deal.
Moody's second consecutive downgrade (November 2024) explicitly cited the political transition and
banking-sector fragility, not macro fundamentals alone -- a rating agency stating outright that politics
had become the first-order credit driver. Accountability proceedings continued into the interim period:
Hasina was convicted in absentia of crimes against humanity by the International Crimes Tribunal in
November 2025 for the July 2024 crackdown (by then estimated to have killed roughly 1,400 people) and
sentenced to death, a ruling she denounced from exile in India. The 13th parliamentary elections and a
constitutional-reform referendum were scheduled for February 12, 2026 -- the first national vote since
Hasina's ouster; Shahabuddin's mid-2026 resignation and Alamgir's August 2026 parliamentary election as his
successor indicate the political order that emerged from that vote is still actively settling.

**Case-specific judgments:**
- *(Moderate-high confidence)* Bangladesh is the clearest case among the three where political rupture
  preceded rather than followed the most acute fiscal stress -- a full-blown balance-of-payments crisis was
  not a precondition for the government's fall.
- *(High confidence)* A rating agency (Moody's, November 2024) explicitly naming political transition
  ahead of macro fundamentals as the credit driver is a rare, direct confirmation of the fiscal-political
  feedback loop from inside the market's own risk-pricing mechanism, not just from qualitative narrative.
- *(Moderate confidence)* The February 2026 election did not close the political-transition chapter --
  a second head-of-state change (Shahabuddin to Alamgir) by mid/late 2026 suggests continued institutional
  churn rather than a settled post-Hasina order, worth tracking further in a future issue of this project's
  MENA-adjacent monthly series or a dedicated follow-up.

## Cross-Case Synthesis

Having read the three cases in sequence rather than as a blended finding, three patterns emerge that a
single unified narrative would have obscured:

**1. The same underlying stress resolves differently depending on whether an external patron exists.**
Sri Lanka had no external backer willing to indefinitely bridge its financing gap and let reserves run out
entirely before restructuring. Pakistan has three (China, Saudi Arabia, the UAE) willing to keep rolling
over bilateral debt rather than forcing a reckoning. Bangladesh's political system broke under an unrelated
grievance before its fiscal deterioration reached Sri Lanka-level severity, making the external-patron
question moot for the political outcome (though still relevant to its ongoing IMF relationship).

**2. Three competing framings for how fiscal crisis and political instability relate, tested against the
cases above:**

- **Framing A -- Fiscal crisis drives instability** (the classic IMF-austerity-riot model): debt distress
  and its remedies generate the hardship that produces unrest and regime change. *Best fits Sri Lanka* --
  the chain from default to Aragalaya to two presidential exits is direct, fast, and well-documented.
- **Framing B -- Instability drives fiscal crisis**: authoritarian overreach and repression are the root
  cause; economic deterioration compounds the resulting instability without originating it. *Best fits
  Bangladesh* -- the proximate trigger (a hiring-quota grievance and a violent crackdown) had no direct
  fiscal content, even as reserve depletion had already begun eroding the government's credibility.
- **Framing C -- Chronic, mutually reinforcing, no clean causal priority**: fiscal fragility and governance
  weakness compound each other repeatedly over years without a single trigger resolving (or ending) the
  relationship. *Best fits Pakistan* -- fourteen years of recurring crisis-and-management cycles (2013,
  2019, 2022, 2023, 2024, plus two new 2025 geopolitical shocks) with no single point where one factor
  clearly caused the other, and no resolution, just continuation.

No single framing fits all three cases. That is itself the finding, not a failure to find one.

**3. Political-stability indicators alone are a weaker predictor of near-term crisis outcome than debt
trajectory and reserve buffers combined.** Pakistan's WGI political-stability reading is far worse than
either Sri Lanka's or Bangladesh's current figures, yet Pakistan is the one of the three that has never
defaulted -- reserve cover and external rollover-partner reliability explain more of the divergence in
outcome than the governance indicator does on its own. (Three cases is not a statistically robust sample;
this is a pattern worth testing against more of the MENASA Risk Monitor's other 31 countries, not a proven
relationship.)

## Implications for Regional Stability and Western Strategic Interests

- **IMF conditionality's effectiveness depends on a government's political capacity to sustain it, which
  the Fund's own frameworks don't score.** Pakistan's rating upgrades mid-program alongside deepening
  military constitutional primacy is the clearest evidence that "on-track with the IMF" and "improving
  governance" are separable, sometimes divergent outcomes.
- **China's creditor posture differs meaningfully between the two cases where it matters most.** In
  Pakistan, China acts as a rollover lender of last resort, repeatedly extending rather than restructuring
  bilateral debt. In Sri Lanka, China participated in a formal, coordinated restructuring alongside Japan,
  India, and France via the Official Creditor Committee. Same creditor, two different postures --worth
  tracking as a leading indicator of how Beijing might treat future distressed borrowers elsewhere.
- **Pakistan's 2025 conflicts add a genuinely new risk channel Western policymakers should track
  separately from its fiscal story.** A four-day India exchange and an ongoing Afghan border conflict are
  not IMF-program risk in any conventional sense, but both demonstrably move Pakistani market pricing
  (the KSE-100's 13% cumulative fall) and trade flows -- a reminder that sovereign risk assessment focused
  only on debt/reserves/ratings will miss geopolitical shocks that arrive on a completely different timeline.
- **Bangladesh's export concentration gives trade policy outsized leverage over its post-crisis
  trajectory.** RMG's roughly 80% export share meant the negotiated US tariff reduction (35% to 19-20%,
  signed Feb 2026) mattered more to its recovery path than most domestic policy choices available to its
  interim, then newly-reconstituted, government.
- **A purely quantitative early-warning model would have missed Bangladesh's trigger and been slow on
  Pakistan's 2025 conflicts.** This has a direct methodological implication for the MENASA Risk Monitor
  itself: the backward-looking, annual composite score is a reasonable screening tool but not a leading
  indicator for either abrupt political rupture or fast-moving geopolitical shocks -- the tool's own Live
  Conflicts tab (qualitative, more frequently updated) is the more appropriate complement for both purposes.

## Outlook: Indicators to Watch

- **Whether Bangladesh's political order stabilizes** following Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir's August 2026
  parliamentary election as president -- a second head-of-state change within two years of Hasina's ouster
  suggests the post-2024 order is still settling, not settled; the near-term test is whether a third
  transition follows or the current arrangement holds.
- **Whether Pakistan's IMF-anchored rating upgrades continue** as the 27th Amendment's military
  constitutional primacy is tested by the next domestic flashpoint, and whether the India and Afghanistan
  conflict tracks reopen or stay frozen.
- **Sri Lanka's continuing IMF review cycle and whether the NPP supermajority translates into durable
  reform** rather than the power-concentration pattern seen in Pakistan -- a real test of whether a landslide
  mandate this large produces a genuinely different governance trajectory.
- **Any further China, Saudi, or UAE rollover requirement for Pakistan** before its current EFF concludes
  -- recurrence reinforces the "chronic, unresolved" Case I characterization; a genuine reduction in
  rollover dependency would weaken it.
- **Whether Bangladesh's RMG-dependent trade relationship with Washington remains a stabilizing lever** or
  becomes a new point of leverage/friction as the political situation continues to evolve.

## Methodology and Limitations

This assessment's quantitative layer (risk scores, debt-to-GDP, governance indicators, reserves, credit
ratings, current-government records) is drawn directly from the MENASA Risk Monitor's `scored_data.csv`,
`CREDIT_RATINGS`, and `CURRENT_GOVERNMENT` data. Its qualitative layer -- the IMF-program histories, the
Aragalaya and Bangladesh-uprising narratives, the 2025 Pakistan conflict episodes, the creditor-relationship
summaries -- is drawn from that tool's curated `context_data.py`, itself sourced from IMF press releases,
Reuters/Bloomberg/AP wire reporting, and rating-agency actions, fact-checked per that tool's own methodology
note. This revision draws on substantially more of that underlying dataset than the first version of this
brief -- all 14 of Pakistan's historical-context entries, all 12 of Sri Lanka's, and all 13 of Bangladesh's
informed the case narratives above, not a partial subset.

**This brief inherits the Risk Monitor's own stated limitations.** Its composite score is annual and
backward-looking by design (see Implications, above, for where that specifically mattered in Bangladesh's
and Pakistan's cases); debt-to-GDP coverage is sparse and reference years vary by country (Sri Lanka's
cited figure dates to 2015; readers should treat cross-country debt comparisons here as directional, not
perfectly time-matched). The Risk Monitor's own README states its weights are "a transparent, reasonable
starting point -- not a backtested or econometrically validated model," a caveat that applies with equal
force to any conclusion drawn from its data here.

**This brief examines three of the region's eight South Asian economies in depth** (Pakistan, Sri Lanka,
Bangladesh), chosen because the underlying dataset's qualitative depth concentrates on the countries that
have experienced the most acute fiscal-political stress in the period covered -- not because India, Nepal,
Bhutan, Maldives, and Afghanistan are less analytically interesting. A future pass extending the same
case-study depth to Nepal's frequent government turnover and the Maldives' India-China geopolitical debt
tension would be a natural next step for this project.
